# Black Holes

## Overview

A black hole is a region of spacetime where gravity is so extreme that nothing can escape after crossing the event horizon — not even light.

They are among the most scientifically well-documented extreme objects in the universe, confirmed through multiple independent observations.

---

# Formation

## Stellar Black Holes

Form when massive stars (roughly eight or more times the mass of the Sun) exhaust their nuclear fuel, collapse under gravity, and explode as supernovae. If the remaining core exceeds about three solar masses, it collapses into a black hole.

## Direct Collapse

In rare cases, very massive stars may collapse directly into black holes without a visible supernova.

---

# Structure

## Singularity

The theoretical center where matter is compressed to infinite density under current physics. General relativity breaks down here, meaning this region is not fully understood.

## Event Horizon

The boundary of no return. Once matter or light crosses the event horizon, it cannot escape. The radius of the event horizon for a non-rotating black hole is called the Schwarzschild radius.

## Photon Sphere

Just outside the event horizon, light can orbit the black hole in unstable circular paths.

## Accretion Disk

Hot, rapidly rotating material spiraling toward the black hole. Friction heats it to millions of degrees, emitting X-rays and other radiation — making black holes indirectly observable.

---

# Types of Black Holes

## Stellar Black Holes

Formed from individual massive stars. Typically range from about 5 to several dozen solar masses.

Confirmed through gravitational wave detections by LIGO/VIRGO and X-ray binary observations.

## Intermediate-Mass Black Holes (IMBHs)

Theorized to exist between stellar and supermassive black holes, with masses from hundreds to hundreds of thousands of solar masses. Evidence is growing but not yet conclusive.

## Supermassive Black Holes

Found at the centers of most large galaxies. Range from millions to billions of solar masses.

Our galaxy's central black hole, Sagittarius A*, has a mass of approximately 4 million solar masses.

---

# Famous Examples

## Sagittarius A* (Sgr A*)

Located at the center of the Milky Way, approximately 26,000 light-years from Earth.

Its existence was confirmed over decades of stellar orbit observations. The first direct image was published by the Event Horizon Telescope in 2022.

## M87*

Located in galaxy Messier 87, approximately 55 million light-years away.

The first-ever direct image of a black hole's shadow was captured in 2019 by the Event Horizon Telescope, providing visual confirmation of event horizon structure.

# Hawking Radiation

Theoretical physicist Stephen Hawking proposed in 1974 that black holes are not perfectly black — they slowly emit thermal radiation due to quantum effects near the event horizon.

This radiation has not yet been directly detected because it is extraordinarily faint for stellar-mass or larger black holes.

Hawking radiation implies that black holes very slowly lose mass and, over enormous timescales, could eventually evaporate completely.

This creates the **black hole information paradox**: if a black hole evaporates, does the information about what fell in survive or is it destroyed?

This remains one of the deepest unresolved problems in theoretical physics and a rich storytelling opportunity.

---

# Gravitational Waves

When two black holes merge, they produce gravitational waves — ripples in spacetime.

The first confirmed detection was made by LIGO on September 14, 2015 (event GW150914), directly confirming the existence of stellar-mass black hole binary systems.

---

# Scientific Mysteries

* What happens to information at the singularity? (Information paradox)
* How do supermassive black holes grow so large so quickly in the early universe?
* Do intermediate-mass black holes exist in large numbers?
* Can Hawking radiation ever be detected?

* Jet structures above and below the poles (for active supermassive black holes)
